How this app works

A plain-language guide to classifying comments, understanding ham vs spam, and reading your results.

What this app does

You paste a short comment—usually the kind posted on video sites—and the app estimates whether it is ham (a normal, on-topic message) or spam (unwanted promotional or misleading junk). The guess comes from a machine-learning model trained on thousands of real comments that people already labelled as ham or spam.

The model does not browse the web or look up your account. It only sees the text you submit and returns a label plus confidence scores for each class.

Ham vs spam — what’s the difference?

These terms come from email filtering, but they apply to comments too:

  • Ham is legitimate content: reactions, questions, praise, criticism, jokes, or conversation that belongs on the thread. It might be short (“lol”) or long, positive or negative—the key is that it is not trying to trick you into clicks, scams, or off-site promotions.
  • Spam is unwanted noise, often repeated at scale: “subscribe to my channel,” fake giveaways, phishing links, SEO bait, or copy-pasted ads that have nothing to do with the video. Spam is about manipulation or promotion, not genuine discussion.

The boundary is not always obvious. A comment can mention a product sincerely (ham) or push a link aggressively (spam). Sarcasm, slang, and other languages can confuse any classifier. Treat the output as a probability, not a final verdict.

What happens when you click Classify

  1. Tokenize — Your text is split into words and mapped to a fixed vocabulary (the 2,000 most common terms seen during training).
  2. TF-IDF vector — Each comment becomes a numeric vector: important words in this comment get higher weight; words that appear everywhere get lower weight. The vector is scaled and centered so values sit in a stable range for the network.
  3. Neural network — A small feed-forward model (one large hidden layer with dropout, then two outputs) estimates the chance of ham vs spam.
  4. Result — The higher probability wins. The UI shows both percentages so you can see how confident the model is.

Training and “Model ready”

On first launch (or after the saved model is removed), the server trains on labelled YouTube-style comments bundled with the app. Training runs in the background so the home page can load immediately; the status line turns green when the model is ready.

A holdout slice of the data is kept back during training to measure accuracy. That percentage on the home page is how well the model did on comments it had not seen during training—not a guarantee on your specific text.

After training finishes, weights are saved locally on the server so later starts load in seconds instead of retraining.

Limitations and responsible use

  • Works best on short, informal English comments similar to the training set.
  • Can mislabel edgy jokes, quotes, or benign links that look promotional.
  • Should not be the only tool for moderation; humans and policies still matter.
  • Do not paste passwords, private messages, or personal data you would not share publicly.

Example chips on the home page are only shortcuts—you can type any comment you like.
